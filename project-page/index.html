The idea in 90 seconds

A reversible map P moves the data to a representation where the flow's intermediate distributions are well conditioned; the flow is trained there, and samples are mapped back with P−1. Both a single anisotropic Gaussian and a six-mode mixture are carried through the same four steps, and compared with standard FM at an identical budget. Abstract

Geometric optimization, not model expressiveness, is the bottleneck

Flow matching learns vector fields by regressing stochastic velocity targets along intermediate distributions \(p_t\). We identify a geometric optimization bottleneck in this regression problem: when the covariance \(\Sigma_t\) of \(p_t\) is ill-conditioned, gradient-based training rapidly fits high-variance directions while making slow progress along low-variance ones.

In an exactly solvable Gaussian setting, we prove that the excess risk is weighted by \(\Sigma_t\), and that both gradient descent and stochastic gradient descent inherit condition-number-dependent convergence. We then extend the analysis to Gaussian mixtures, showing that multimodality does not average away this effect; instead, the slowest and worst-conditioned component can control optimization.

Motivated by this analysis, we propose preconditioned flow matching, a precondition-then-match framework that transforms the target distribution into a more isotropic representation, trains the main flow in the transformed space, and maps generated samples back through the inverse transformation. We show theoretically that preconditioning reshapes the intermediate FM path and improves its conditioning. Across controlled Gaussian and Gaussian-mixture experiments, latent MNIST and other high-resolution image datasets up to 512×512, preconditioning improves path-conditioning diagnostics, low-eigenvalue recovery, FID, MMD, precision and recall. Compute-matched baselines and preconditioner-quality ablations further show that the gains are not explained merely by additional preconditioner parameters, but by improved geometry of the downstream flow matching problem.

Three observations

Why a small loss can hide a bad model

01

Excess risk is weighted by \(\Sigma_t\)

For linear predictors the population risk splits exactly into an irreducible floor and a quadratic form in the covariance of the intermediate distribution.

$$L_t(A) - L_t^{\star} = \mathrm{Tr}\!\left[(A - A^{\star})\,\Sigma_t\,(A - A^{\star})^{\top}\right]$$

Where \(\sigma_i(t)\) is small, a loss below \(\varepsilon\) still permits a velocity error up to \(\sqrt{\varepsilon/\sigma_i(t)}\).

02

Convergence scales with \(\kappa(\Sigma_t)\)

Gradient descent on the same quadratic converges linearly, but the iteration count to reduce the error by ε grows with the condition number of the intermediate covariance.

$$k = O\!\left(\kappa(\Sigma_t)\cdot\log(1/\varepsilon)\right)$$

At a fixed \(t\) the conditioning is

$$\kappa(\Sigma_t) = \frac{(1-t)^2 + t^2\lambda_{\max}}{(1-t)^2 + t^2\lambda_{\min}}$$

with \(\lambda\) the eigenvalues of the target covariance.

Scalar time reweighting rescales the loss but cannot remove this directional stiffness.

03

Mixtures do not average it away

With component-wise dynamics, the whole mixture is governed by the slowest eigendirection across all components — one badly conditioned mode can set the time scale for the entire model.

$$\mathrm{rate} \;\propto\; \min_{k,i}\,\sigma_{k,i}(t)$$

Anisotropy gets amplified rather than diluted by multimodality.

Method

Precondition, match, map back

Preconditioning does not change the generative model or its sampling ODE. It changes the representation in which the flow is trained, so that the regression problem it solves is well conditioned.

Step 1

Precondition

Learn a reversible map P (normalizing flow, or a low-capacity flow model) that pushes the target toward an isotropic representation:

$$\tilde{x}_1 = P(x_1), \qquad \mathrm{Cov}(\tilde{x}_1) \approx I$$

Step 2

Match

Train the unchanged FM objective on the transformed path, from the same Gaussian reference. With the sincos schedule used in every experiment here, \(c(t)=\cos(\pi t/2)\), \(s(t)=\sin(\pi t/2)\):

$$\tilde{x}_t = \cos\!\frac{\pi t}{2}\,x_0 + \sin\!\frac{\pi t}{2}\,\tilde{x}_1, \qquad x_0 \sim \mathcal{N}(0, I)$$

Step 3

Map back

Integrate the learned ODE and invert the preconditioner. Nothing else changes at sampling time:

$$\hat{x}_1 = P^{-1}\!\left(\hat{\tilde{x}}_1\right)$$

Algorithm 1 — Precondition-then-Match

  1. Learn a reversible preconditioner \(P_\phi\) on target data, so that \(\mathrm{Cov}(P_\phi(x_1)) \approx I\).
  2. Transform the targets: \(\tilde{x}_1 = P_\phi(x_1)\).
  3. Train \(v_\theta\) with the unchanged FM objective on the preconditioned path \(\tilde{x}_t = \cos\!\frac{\pi t}{2}\,x_0 + \sin\!\frac{\pi t}{2}\,\tilde{x}_1\).
  4. Sample \(z \sim \mathcal{N}(0,I)\) and solve \(d\tilde{x}/dt = v_\theta(\tilde{x}, t)\) to obtain \(\hat{\tilde{x}}_1\).
  5. Return \(\hat{x}_1 = P_\phi^{-1}(\hat{\tilde{x}}_1)\).

Conditioning in real representations

What we do not claim

  • Preconditioning is not a new backbone, sampler or loss: the downstream FM model and its ODE are unchanged.
  • It does not mainly accelerate the early drop of the loss; it prevents optimization from plateauing along suppressed directions.
  • It is complementary to latent modelling, optimal flow matching and schedule design.
